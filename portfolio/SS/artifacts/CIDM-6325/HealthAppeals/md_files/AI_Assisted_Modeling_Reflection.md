# Reflection on AI-Assisted Modeling in Django
As I worked through the blog design portions of the course alongside Dr. Babb’s instructional videos, I found myself regularly turning to AI tools like ChatGPT and GitHub Copilot. As someone new to web development, these tools were a major support system, both technically and conceptually. Whether I was troubleshooting an error or trying to understand how Django’s models and forms work, AI helped supplement my learning alongside Matt’s book and other course materials. That said, while AI often helped me move faster and learn more efficiently, it also came with limitations that required me to step back and think critically about what I was implementing.

One helpful use of AI was in generating Django code that followed best practices. GitHub Copilot was incredibly useful for learning the features of Visual Studio Code. It offered suggestions in real time, especially when I encountered error messages or incomplete code, and it gave me options, whether to accept the suggestion as is or modify it. However, I also learned that relying too much on Copilot could be a hindrance. If I was not careful to fully understand what the AI was suggesting, I risked implementing changes I could not explain, justify, or know what trickle down effects it would have.

One unexpected but important benefit of using AI was how it helped me navigate platform differences. Since Dr. Babb used a Mac and me a Windows machine, there were times when instructions did not align with my setup such as when it came to terminal commands or managing Python environments. ChatGPT and Github Copilot made it easy to bridge that gap. I could ask, “What’s the Windows equivalent of this Mac command?” or “How do I set environment variables in Windows?” and get quick, concise answers. Without that assistance, I would have lost time trying to translate those differences on my own.

AI assistance did not have without its hindrances, however. One recurring issue was that it sometimes suggested overly complex solutions that did not align with what we were doing in class. These advanced suggestions were not necessarily wrong, but they were often out of scope for the course and my ability. I had several moments where I had to remind myself that just because AI made a suggestion, it does not mean it is the appropriate one.

In the end, I have come to see AI as a helpful guide, a supplemental tool that works best when used in combination with my own judgment, course materials, and instructor guidance. It helped me debug, bridge technical gaps, and better understand core concepts. But ultimately, it is my responsibility to ensure that the code I write reflects both the assignment’s expectations and my own understanding.
